# Build-up Play

## What build-up play is

Build-up play is the phase in which a team in possession moves the ball from its own
defensive third towards the middle third with the aim of creating a favourable situation
for progression. It usually begins with the goalkeeper or the centre-backs after a goal
kick, a back pass or a regain of possession deep in the team's own half.

The main objective is not simply to keep the ball. A good build-up creates an advantage:
a free player between the opponent's lines, a numerical superiority in one zone, or a
positional advantage that lets a player receive facing forward.

## Common structures

Teams organise the first line of build-up in different shapes. Typical examples are:

- **Back two with a single pivot.** The two centre-backs split wide and one central
  midfielder drops in front of them, forming a triangle.
- **Back three.** A third player joins the centre-backs. This can be a natural
  third centre-back, a full-back who stays narrow, or a midfielder who drops between
  the centre-backs.
- **Back four with a double pivot.** Two central midfielders offer short options in
  front of the back line, which gives security against counter-attacks after a loss.

The choice of structure often depends on how many players the opponent commits to the
first pressing line. A common principle is to have one more player in the first line of
build-up than the opponent has in its first pressing line.

## Key principles

**Width and depth.** Players spread across the pitch to stretch the opponent. Wide
players hold the touchline and a forward pins the opponent's back line, creating space
between the lines for midfielders.

**Third-man combinations.** When a direct pass to a player is blocked, the ball can be
played to a second player who then finds the third, free player. This is one of the most
reliable ways to break a compact press.

**Body orientation.** A player who receives with an open body shape can see more of the
pitch and play forward quickly. Coaches often teach receiving on the back foot for this
reason.

**Patience and tempo changes.** Circulating the ball can move the opponent's block and
create a gap. The moment a passing lane opens, the tempo should increase so that the
opponent cannot recover its shape.

## Risks

Build-up from the back carries risk. A loss of possession close to the own goal can
lead directly to a scoring chance for the opponent. Teams therefore usually define
clear alternatives:

- a longer pass to a target forward when short options are covered;
- a switch of play to the weak side;
- rest-defence positions, so that players behind the ball are ready to defend if the
  ball is lost.

## What data can and cannot show

Event data records passes, receptions and their locations, so it can show where a team
progressed the ball and which players were frequently involved. It cannot by itself show
the positions of all players when a pass was made. Questions about spacing, body
orientation or the exact shape of the build-up structure normally require tracking data
or video review.

## Related concepts

Build-up play is closely related to the opponent's pressing strategy (see high pressing),
to rest defence, and to defensive transitions after a loss of possession in the first
or second third of the pitch.
